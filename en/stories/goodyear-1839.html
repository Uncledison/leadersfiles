01The valve that was refused

In 1834 Goodyear took an air valve for a life jacket to the Roxbury Rubber Company in New York. His father had failed in the hardware trade in New Haven, and he was looking for something he could sell. The company refused the valve without looking at it. The clerk told him instead to think of a way to make rubber usable.

That clerk took him to the storeroom. The shelves were stacked with rubber goods melted together by the summer heat. India rubber ran in summer and cracked in winter. The American rubber boom of the mid-1830s was going out that way.

Goodyear spent the 5 years after that storeroom on rubber. He mixed chemicals in pots and frying pans in his wife's kitchen and in the kitchen of the prison he entered because of debt: nitric acid, lime, turpentine. The household goods were sold one by one.

021837, losing everything

In 1836 he succeeded in smoothing the surface of rubber with nitric acid. The United States Post Office in Boston ordered rubber mailbags from him. When summer came the bags melted again. The acid had hardened only the surface.

In 1837 the United States fell into a financial panic. Goodyear's family lost what was left. He fished to feed them. The mailbags melted, the investors left, and he went to prison again.

He is said to have called the debtors' prison a ‘hotel’. It was not a joke; there is a record that he said it. Even in prison at night he mixed rubber in dishes borrowed from the kitchen.

03The stove in Woburn

In 1839 Goodyear was showing his rubber in a shop in Woburn, Massachusetts. People laughed. He swung his hand in his excitement, and the lump of rubber flew onto the stove.

The rubber he scraped off did not melt and stick. The surface was scorched like leather, and at the edge a fully hardened rim was left. He spent 5 more years heating rubber mixed with sulphur over stoves, in ovens, with irons and with kettles.

In June 1844 he received US patent 3,633 for treating rubber with sulphur at high heat. The process came to be called vulcanisation, after the Roman god of fire.

04Poor even with the patent

The patent did not make him rich. Companies that copied the process appeared in the United States and Europe, and he spent his life in lawsuits. The European patents were voided on technical grounds. He lost a libel case too, and went to prison again in France.

At the Paris Exposition of 1855 Napoleon III meant to give him the Légion d’honneur. Goodyear was in a debtors' prison outside Paris. His son brought the medal to the prison. He was held 16 days and then released.

On 1 July 1860, on his way to see his sick daughter, he collapsed in New York. The daughter had already died. He died at 59, and his estate was about $200,000 of debt.

05What he left

When he died, the industry using vulcanised rubber employed 60,000 people and earned $8,000,000 a year. His name today belongs to one of the largest tyre companies in the world, but that company is not one he founded. The company that borrowed his name was set up 38 years after his death.

Goodyear was not a man who overcame failure. He was a man who kept experimenting inside it. What he had was not money but the persistence to look for a compound that would not melt, and that persistence turned one accident into 5 years of experiment.
