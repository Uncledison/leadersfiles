On 10 March 1902 Henry Ford walked out of the company that carried his name. In his hands were $900, the right to keep using his own name, and the drawings of a large racing car. The company dropped his name and became Cadillac. 15 months later he opened again with 12 investors and $28,000.

01The first company, at 36

On 5 August 1899 the Detroit Automobile Company was set up with money from William Murphy, a Detroit timber magnate. Ford was works manager on $150 a month. He had left a secure post at the Edison Illuminating Company that same month, turning down a salary of $1,900.

In January 1900 the first delivery vehicle was finished, and the demonstration on the street went well. But the shareholders wanted several models sold quickly for a profit, and Ford had never built more than 1 car at a time. The cars fell short of the quality he wanted and cost too much.

He took $75 as his last pay on 29 October 1900. The company was formally dissolved in January 1901.

02The second company, in his own name

In October 1901 Ford drove a 26-horsepower racer and won a 10-mile race. On 30 November that year Murphy and the old shareholders set up the Henry Ford Company. Ford was chief engineer and received one sixth of the stock, valued at $100,000.

The company's aim was a light 2-seater at about $1,000. Ford was absorbed in designing a larger 4-cylinder racer. Murphy, at the end of his patience, brought in the famous machinist Henry Leland as a consultant. On 10 March 1902 Ford left.

The settlement gave him $900, the continuing right to use his name, and the drawings of the racer he had designed. The company soon renamed itself the Cadillac Automobile Company. The second company, which had carried his name, survived without him as Cadillac.

03The third was not built alone

Ford went to Alexander Malcomson, a coal dealer. On 20 August 1902 the two signed the ‘Ford and Malcomson’ agreement. Parts for the new car were ordered from the shop of the machinist brothers John and Horace Dodge. The orders came to more than $160,000.

By February 1903 the money was gone and the Dodges wanted paying. Malcomson brought in his uncle John Gray, a bank president. With Gray's name attached, local merchants and lawyers invested; the Dodges agreed to take shares instead of cash.

On 16 June 1903 the Ford Motor Company was incorporated. The capital was $28,000 in cash plus about $21,000 promised by the other investors. There were 12 shareholders and 1,000 shares: 255 each for Ford and Malcomson, 105 for Gray. Ford was not trusted to run the company, so Gray became president.

04What $28,000 did

The first car was the Model A of 1903. The company sold nearly 1,700 in its first year. The Model T appeared in October 1908 at $825, and 15,000,000 were sold over 19 years.

In 1913 at the Highland Park plant Ford developed assembly with the car drawn along the floor. Assembly time fell sharply and the price fell further. In January 1914 he raised the daily wage to $5, among the highest in the American car industry at the time.

He was fixed on making the car cheaper. A lower price meant more buyers, and more buyers meant a lower price again. That calculation was what he held on to to the end at the third company.

05The company closed twice

To avoid repeating the failure, Ford kept 2 things. The first was his name: when he left the second company, the right to use it went into the settlement. The second was the drawings: the design of the car he wanted to build stayed with him, not with the company.

He is reported to have said later: “I will build a motor car for the great multitude.” The line is widely quoted as a declaration of about 1908, when the Model T appeared. What actually let him build that car was not the sentence but the name and the drawings he carried out of a company that closed.

One line of courage

The company closed twice.
His name did not close.

The settlement of 1902 included the right to his name, and on 16 June 1903 he started again with $28,000.
