01That day he was in the mountains

On 18 September 1873 the Philadelphia bank Jay Cooke & Company stopped payment. It had financed American railways after the Civil War. The New York Stock Exchange closed, and a depression that lasted for years began that day.

Carnegie was then at Cresson in the Allegheny Mountains of Pennsylvania, at the summer house with his family, when the telegram came. He later wrote in his autobiography: “I then entered upon the most anxious period of my business life.”

Telegrams brought news every hour. When one firm fell, the firms that had money with it fell too. His worry, he wrote, was not the bills his company owed but the money owed to it: “It was not bills payable that troubled us, but bills receivable.”

02A works standing half built

A year earlier Carnegie had returned from seeing the Bessemer process in England and decided to make railway rails from steel, not iron. He set up a new company and raised $700,000. $250,000 of it was his.

On 13 April 1873, ground was broken on 106 acres along the Monongahela River, 11 miles from Pittsburgh. Digging the foundations turned up bayonets and blades buried from General Braddock's battle of 1755. The failure of September stopped the construction.

Several partners who had promised funds could not pay their share. Carnegie bought their interests outright and moved the loss onto his own side. Control of the works came into his hands that way, and so did a larger debt.

He did not help Tom Scott. Scott had brought him into the Pennsylvania Railroad and was his mentor and patron, and at that moment he had staked a large sum on the Texas Pacific Railroad. Carnegie made it known to the banks that not $1 of his name stood on that company's paper. Scott failed, and their friendship ended there.

Whether refusing was coldness is still argued. What he protected was one thing: that only his own name stood on his own company's paper. Without that there would have been no next step.

03$400,000 borrowed in London

Rebuilding the works needed money, and American banks were frozen. In the summer of 1874 Carnegie went to London with Alexander Holley, who had designed the plant. The man they met was the international financier Junius Morgan, father of the later J. P. Morgan.

With Morgan's name attached, the bonds sold. The two issued $400,000 of bonds in London. When the panic had closed the bond market at home, he went to the city where the market was open.

In this period he put cost accounting at the centre of running the company: who saved material, who wasted it, which process produced the best result, all kept as numbers. It was the point at which the firm stopped, as he later put it, being “two moles burrowing in the dark”.

0422 August 1875

On 22 August 1875 the first heat went into the furnace at the Edgar Thomson works. Air was blown through the Bessemer converter, the carbon burned, and a column of flame rose. The first steel was poured into moulds.

The first rail was rolled on 1 September. In September alone the works made a little over 1,200 tons of rail and a profit of $11,000. In 1880 the profit for the year was $1,625,000, more than the whole cost of building the plant.

Carnegie did not sell the works. He knew the railways wanted steel rails, and he knew he could make them cheaper than anyone else. “I believe in the ability to make cheaper than others, and I am not afraid of the result of a sharp fight.” The line survives from his letter to William Shinn of November 1875.

05What the panic taught

The failure of 1873 left Carnegie with one works. He stood at the centre of American steel for the next 25 years, and in 1901 sold the company for $480,000,000. The money went to libraries, universities and concert halls.

What he learned from the panic was not optimism. It was telling apart those who could pay from those who could not, keeping debt off his own name, and looking for another market when one closed.

He later wrote: “Watch the costs and the profits will take care of themselves.” It is the shortest version of what he learned at Cresson in 1873.
