At 5:15 pm on 9 December 1914, fire broke out in the film inspection room in West Orange, New Jersey. Records and prototypes built up over 30 years turned to ash that night. The next morning, the 67-year-old man stood on the same ground.

01That night he called people in
to watch it burn

The fire began in the film inspection room. At 5:15 pm on 9 December 1914, Building 41 of the West Orange complex. Nitrocellulose, then the base of motion picture film, was made from the same family of chemicals as gunpowder. When 1 reel burned, the chemical storehouse next door threw out fire in turn.

By 6:20 pm, 7 buildings were burning. By 7:40 pm, 13 were burning at once. The company's own fire brigade of 72 men and engines from neighbouring towns fought it, but an explosion at 9:30 pm threw flame 30 metres into the air. Records put the crowd that came to watch that night at 10,000 people.

The buildings were reinforced concrete. Because he believed them fireproof, he had not insured them fully. The loss came to almost $1,000,000; insurance returned about one third of it.

His son Charles was there. He was 24. He later recalled his father's face.

“Go get your mother and all her friends. They'll never see a fire like this again.”
— Charles Edison, recollection, 1961

When his son tried to hold him back, Edison added: “It's all right. We've just got rid of a lot of rubbish.” That night he told a reporter at the site: “Although I am over 67 years old, I'll start all over again tomorrow.”

His wife Mina Edison led firefighters into her husband's office and carried out instruments, notebooks and drawings. What could be moved before the flames reached the laboratory was moved.

02How he counted failure

Instead of counting failures, he counted the ways that did not work. A 1910 biography quotes his long-time associate Walter Mallory. After more than 9,000 experiments on a nickel-iron storage battery had produced nothing, Mallory expressed his sympathy, and Edison brushed it aside.

“Results? Why, man, I have gotten a lot of results. I know several thousand things that won't work.”

Failure was not a feeling to him but a list. The list was not erased, so the next calculation went faster. The line most often quoted online today comes from the same attitude: “I have not failed. I've just found 10,000 ways that won't work.” It is a later compression of the 1910 testimony, not a recorded sentence of its own; the source note below sets out what is documented.

033 things a man starting again did

1. He wrote everything down. During the fire, employees carried research material and papers out beside the flames. What was lost was the record; what made a restart possible was also the record. Memory burns; what is written can be recalculated.

2. He called the loss ‘cleaning up’. Call the same event an end and the body stiffens first. Call it ‘we got rid of the rubbish’ and the next task becomes visible. He was not being optimistic about the situation; he chose the language that would let him choose the next action.

3. He promised the next morning and kept it. Recovery was a schedule, not a resolution. He laid off none of his employees and put 1,500 people to clearing the rubble. 3 weeks later, on New Year's Day, the plant was partly running again.

04The next day, at 67

The Edison we remember is the man who made the light bulb. But what he repeated until the end was not invention but rebuilding: the phonograph, electric light, motion pictures, the storage battery. What he built was not a single product but a factory that starts again.

One of the first telegrams to arrive after the fire came from Nikola Tesla: “I send you my condolences on behalf of millions of your admirers. This is not a personal loss but a loss to the world.” The two had argued for years over electric current, but that night the sentence arrived first.

When he died on 18 October 1931, 1,093 United States patents carried his name.

05If your night is long

We usually remember the moment of success. What actually changes a person is the morning after a failure. Counting what remains instead of what was lost. That one day decides the next 30 years.

Edison's laboratory stood again. The records he left still switch on lights today.

If yesterday turned to ash,
what today needs is not talent
but showing up.
