0116 June 1858,
a house divided

16 June 1858, the state house in Springfield. The Illinois Republican convention nominated Lincoln for the United States Senate. It was the first time a party formally nominated a Senate candidate at a convention, because the seat was normally chosen by the legislature.

5 sentences into his acceptance speech, Lincoln put in this line: “A house divided against itself cannot stand.” He went on: “I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free.”

Behind it were the Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 and the Dred Scott decision of 1857. His diagnosis was that the conflict over slavery could split the Union. The rest of the speech aimed at Douglas's ‘popular sovereignty’, the argument that the settlers of a territory could decide the question themselves.

02Face to face, 7 times

From 21 August to 15 October that year, Lincoln and Douglas met in 7 Illinois towns: Ottawa, Freeport, Jonesboro, Charleston, Galesburg, Quincy and Alton. The crowds ran from several thousand to more than 10,000.

At Freeport, Lincoln asked Douglas whether the people of a territory could keep slavery out if the Supreme Court ruled they could not. Douglas answered that they could, through their legislature. That answer is the Freeport Doctrine. It angered the South, and in 1860 the Democratic Party split into northern and southern candidates.

Lincoln did not argue for racial equality in the debates. He said that stopping the extension of slavery was the way to preserve the Union. Read by present standards it is an incomplete answer, but what he aimed at was not moral completeness; it was a political boundary.

03He won the votes and lost the seat

The election did not choose a senator directly. Voters chose members of the legislature, and the legislature chose the senator. The Republicans took about 125,000 votes statewide, the Democrats about 121,000.

But because of districts drawn to the 1850 census, the Democrats won more seats. 13 state senators were still serving their terms, and 8 of them were Democrats. Unless the new house gave the Republicans an overwhelming majority, the seat belonged to the Democrats.

On 5 January 1859, the joint ballot went 54 for Douglas and 46 for Lincoln. Lincoln is reported to have said: “It hurts too much to laugh, and I'm too big to cry.”

0419 November 1858,
2 letters

17 days after the defeat, Lincoln wrote 2 letters. One answered his old friend Anson Henry.

“I am glad I made the late race. It gave me a hearing on the great and durable question of the age, which I could have had in no other way; and though I now sink out of view, and shall be forgotten, I believe I have made some marks which will tell for the cause of civil liberty long after I am gone.”
— to Anson G. Henry, 19 November 1858

The same day he wrote to Henry Asbury.

“The fight must go on. The cause of civil liberty must not be surrendered at the end of one, or even, one hundred defeats.”

He was not an optimist. In January 1841, after his engagement broke off, he wrote to his law partner: “I am now the most miserable man living.” Depression followed him all his life. What he won with was not a mood but a schedule: the next election, the next debate, the next letter.

056 November 1860

The presidential election was contested by 4 candidates. Lincoln took 1,866,452 votes, 39.8%, and 180 electoral votes. He finished first in no southern state, but the northern vote decided the presidency.

6 weeks after the election, South Carolina left the Union and the Civil War began. What he had said in 1858 was a calculation, not a prophecy. The house divided did not stand. And rebuilding it fell to him.

In November 1858 he had written that he would sink out of view. 2 years later he stood where the whole country could see him.

From his letter to Henry Asbury, 19 November 1858, 17 days after the defeat.
